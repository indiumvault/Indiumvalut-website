How to plan a revision week

A flexible system for turning a big pile of chapters into small, clear study sessions. Adjust it to your subjects, school timetable, and energy.

1. List the topics, not just the subjects

Instead of writing “study Chemistry,” write a specific target such as “revise atomic structure notes” or “solve five practice questions on a chapter.” A clear task is easier to start and easier to mark complete.

2. Choose priorities realistically

List upcoming tests, unfinished work, and topics that need more practice. Pick a small number of priorities for the week. Leave room for homework, meals, breaks, sleep, and unexpected changes.

A plan is a guide, not a punishment. If a day gets busy, move the task instead of trying to fit everything into one evening.

3. Use short, focused sessions

  1. Choose one topic and a specific goal.
  2. Study the key ideas, then close your notes and explain what you remember.
  3. Try a few practice questions and check the answers.
  4. Write down what still feels unclear so you know what to revisit.

Active recall and practice questions help you check understanding more effectively than only rereading. Take breaks between sessions.

4. Make a simple weekly timetable

Give each day one main study focus and an optional second task. You do not need to fill every hour. Use realistic blocks that fit around school and other responsibilities.

  • Monday–Thursday: revise a small topic or complete a practice set.
  • Friday: revisit the hardest topic from the week.
  • Saturday: catch up on one unfinished priority, if needed.
  • Sunday: review what you learned and prepare a short plan for the next week.

This is only an example. Change the days to match your own schedule and rest needs.

5. Review progress without overcomplicating it

  • Mark a topic complete only after you can explain it or answer practice questions.
  • Keep a short list of doubts to ask a teacher or revisit later.
  • At the end of the week, choose what to keep, change, or remove from next week's plan.

Progress is not the number of hours you sit with a book. Understanding, practice, and consistency matter more than a perfect-looking timetable.
